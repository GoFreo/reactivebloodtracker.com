Why use this as well as your device's own app

Your sensor or meter comes with an app of its own, and that app is the right place for the device's alarms and official features. This app does a different job. It puts glucose from all your devices, your food and your notes on one timeline, so you, your family and your doctors can see why sugar went high or low, not only that it did.

What this app adds

  • Every device on one graph. A device's own app shows that device. Here a FreeStyle Libre, a Dexcom ONE+ (as its connection comes online) and a finger-prick meter sit on the same timeline, and the Compare view shows how closely each sensor followed your finger-pricks.
  • Food lined up with the curve. Meals are marked on the graph, and the Meals view shows what glucose did in the five hours after each one. Carbohydrate comes from the pack's own numbers (barcode, grams, portion), not from a guess.
  • It asks "what happened?" People forget to log things. Sensors don't. When the sensor shows a sudden rise or drop with nothing logged before it, the app asks, and your answer goes on the record.
  • A report for your doctor. The graph and every reading, meal and note for the dates you choose, printed or saved as a file.
  • Your records stay yours. They live on your phone unless you choose to share them. There is no account.

What it does not replace

Keep using your device's own app and its alarms. This app is not an alarm system. It does not replace your sensor or your meter, and it never tells you what to eat or what dose to take.

Go deeper: what your device's own app is for

Abbott describes the FreeStyle Libre 2 system as a continuous glucose monitor with real-time alarms.[[abbott-qrg]] Dexcom's sensor sends a reading to its app or receiver every 5 minutes.[[dexcom-setup]] Those are the official channels for alarms and for decisions about your own treatment.

This app reads your data second-hand, so it can trail the device's own app:

  • Libre: through LibreLinkUp, Abbott's sharing service for family and carers. Abbott says LibreLinkUp is not intended to provide data for treatment decisions.[[abbott-au-faq]] Use what you see here to look back, not to decide.
  • Dexcom: through Dexcom's data service for other apps. Dexcom holds that data back by one hour in the United States and three hours outside it, so three hours in Australia.[[dexcom-api-delay]] The Dexcom line here can be up to three hours behind the Dexcom app.

For anything happening right now, use the device's own app, or a finger-prick.

Go deeper: why food and notes sit beside the glucose line

A sensor knows your glucose. It can't know what you ate, how you felt, or what you were doing. Doctors weigh those together: the Endocrine Society's guideline says a hypoglycaemic disorder should be evaluated when three things are documented. They are symptoms, a low glucose measured at the time, and relief of the symptoms once glucose is raised ("Whipple's triad").[[endo-2009]]

The glucose part is held by your devices. The symptoms and the timing are held by you. Carbohydrate is the main dietary influence on glucose after a meal,[[evert-2019]] so what you ate, and when, is the third piece. Keeping all three on one timeline is the point of this app.

Go deeper: why more than one sensor can help

Two sensors worn together will rarely agree exactly. Dexcom says a sensor reading should fall within 20% of a meter reading when the meter reads 3.9 mmol/L or higher, or within 1.1 mmol/L when it reads lower.[[dexcom-au-mismatch]] Abbott says sensor and blood readings differ most while glucose is changing quickly, and are most alike when it is steady.[[abbott-au-differences]]

Putting both sensors on one graph, with finger-pricks as the reference, lets your own data show which one tracks you more closely. The Compare view reports that agreement. It doesn't tell you which device to use. That is a choice for you and your doctors.

Go deeper: what this app deliberately doesn't do, and why

This app shows what happened. It does not diagnose, it does not suggest a safe portion or a meal plan, and it never suggests medication or an insulin dose. There are three reasons.

  • Safety. A wrong suggestion from an app can hurt someone, and an app can't see your whole situation.
  • Your care team knows you. Your doctor, diabetes educator or dietitian can weigh things this app can't.
  • Regulation. In Australia, software that meets the definition of a medical device is regulated by the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) unless a listed exclusion applies, such as for health self-management software, and every function of an app has to meet the exclusion's conditions.[[tga-exclusions]] Staying descriptive keeps this app on the safe side of that line. Whether any particular feature fits is the TGA's call, not ours, and this isn't legal advice.
