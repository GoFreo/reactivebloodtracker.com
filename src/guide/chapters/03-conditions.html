Reactive hypoglycaemia, diabetes and other conditions

Reactive hypoglycaemia (also called postprandial hypoglycaemia) is low blood sugar that happens after a meal, usually within four hours.[[mayo-reactive]] In people who have diabetes it can be linked to insulin or other glucose-lowering medicine. In people who don't, the cause is often unclear.[[mayo-reactive]]

This app was built first for reactive hypoglycaemia. It also suits people with type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and anyone whose sugar swings after eating, for example after weight-loss surgery.[[mayo-reactive]] It records what happened. Your doctors decide what it means.

Whether a low is a real medical finding is usually decided by looking for three things together: symptoms, a low glucose measured at the time, and relief once glucose comes back up.[[endo-2009]] A record of readings, meals and how you felt helps with exactly that.

Go deeper: reactive hypoglycaemia in more detail

Timing. Mayo Clinic says within about four hours of eating.[[mayo-reactive]] Cleveland Clinic says typically about two hours after a meal and sometimes up to four.[[cleveland-reactive]] An NHS hospital trust gives two to four hours.[[nhs-northtees]]

Causes. In people without diabetes the cause often isn't clear, and Cleveland Clinic says the exact cause is unknown.[[mayo-reactive,cleveland-reactive]] Mayo Clinic lists other possible causes: alcohol, some surgical procedures such as gastric bypass or other weight-loss surgery, inherited metabolic conditions, and certain tumours.[[mayo-reactive]] The NHS trust notes it often follows stomach surgery.[[nhs-northtees]]

Evaluation. Mayo Clinic says a medical evaluation is usually done to see whether symptoms are caused by low blood sugar and whether they ease when blood sugar returns to normal, with more testing if symptoms are severe.[[mayo-reactive]] It also says reactive hypoglycaemia usually doesn't need medical treatment, but that if another condition is causing it, that condition is treated.[[mayo-reactive]] The NHS trust notes some people are referred to an endocrinologist, who may consider medicines.[[nhs-northtees]]

Eating. These pages also describe general eating approaches. This app's Food guidance page (on Home) gathers them with their sources. They are general information, not advice for you. Your own readings, once there are enough of them, are a better guide to what affects you.

Go deeper: a brief dip on a sensor isn't a diagnosis

In a study of 153 people without diabetes, aged 7 to 80, who wore Dexcom G6 sensors for up to 10 days, glucose was below 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) for a median of 1.1% of the time, about 15 minutes a day. Their average glucose was about 5.4 to 5.5 mmol/L for most age groups.[[shah-2019]]

So sensors record brief dips even in people who feel well. That is one reason doctors look at symptoms and a measured low together, not a sensor number alone.[[endo-2009]] It is also why the device makers say to confirm a surprising reading with a finger-prick (see the chapter on sensors and finger-pricks).

Go deeper: diabetes in brief
  • Type 1: an autoimmune condition. The immune system attacks the cells in the pancreas that make insulin, so the body makes little or none, and insulin treatment is needed.[[niddk-diabetes,diabetesaus-types]]
  • Type 2: the body becomes resistant to insulin and gradually loses the ability to make enough. It is the most common type.[[niddk-diabetes,diabetesaus-types]]
  • Gestational diabetes: develops during pregnancy and usually goes after the birth.[[niddk-diabetes]]
  • Prediabetes: glucose higher than normal but not high enough to be called type 2 diabetes.[[niddk-diabetes]]

Lows in diabetes most often come from insulin or certain glucose-lowering medicines, extra activity, delayed or missed meals or too little carbohydrate, alcohol (especially without food), and vomiting or diarrhoea.[[healthdirect-hypo,niddk-hypo]]

If you take insulin or other glucose-lowering medicine, any change to your plan is for your doctor or diabetes educator. This app has no dose calculator and never will.

Go deeper: "time in range", a common way to read sensor data

For people with type 1 or type 2 diabetes, an international consensus of experts recommends aiming for glucose between 3.9 and 10.0 mmol/L (70 to 180 mg/dL) more than 70% of the time. It suggests under 4% of the time below 3.9 mmol/L and under 1% below 3.0 mmol/L, judged on at least 14 days with more than 70% of readings captured.[[tir-2019]]

Those are targets for people with diabetes, set by experts. They are not goals this app sets for you, and they weren't designed for reactive hypoglycaemia in people without diabetes. Ask your doctor which numbers matter for you.

Go deeper: weight-loss surgery and alcohol

After some weight-loss operations, low sugar after meals can occur. Mayo Clinic and an NHS trust both list stomach surgery among the possible causes.[[mayo-reactive,nhs-northtees]] If that is you, tell your care team that you are tracking, and ask what they want to see.

Alcohol is listed as a possible cause of reactive hypoglycaemia.[[mayo-reactive]] In people with diabetes, a consensus report says alcohol can raise the risk of a delayed low, because it inhibits the body's production of new glucose and can reduce awareness of a low, and that drinking with food can reduce the risk of a low overnight.[[evert-2019]] That is why the diary and food notes are worth using for drinks too.
