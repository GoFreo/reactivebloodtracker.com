Food: type it, scan it, photograph it, build it

Food goes in from the Food box on Home. Use whichever way is quickest at the time. Each one ends up as a meal marker on your graph.

  • Type or say it. The fastest way. Good when you only need the gist.
  • Scan a barcode. For packaged food. The label's carbohydrate numbers fill in for you.
  • Photograph the plate. For food with no label. The least exact way, and the app treats it that way.
  • Build it from ingredients. For home cooking. Exact if you weigh what you use.
Go deeper: why record carbohydrate at all?

Carbohydrate is the main dietary influence on blood glucose after a meal. Foods that contain it behave differently: some give a long, slow rise and fall, others a quick rise followed by a quick fall.[[evert-2019]] A consensus report on nutrition for people with diabetes names monitoring carbohydrate intake as a key strategy.[[evert-2019]]

That is why this app records grams of carbohydrate. It is the number on the label, the figure clinicians most often ask about, and the one most linked to the after-meal curve you are looking at. The app doesn't score your foods and never says what you should eat. It puts the carbohydrate beside what your glucose did.

Go deeper: why not just estimate by eye?

Because estimates are often off. In a study of adults with type 1 diabetes, people's own carbohydrate estimates for meals differed from a dietitian's analysis by about 15 g per meal on average, which was about 21% of the meal's carbohydrate. Of the 448 meals studied, 63% were underestimated.[[brazeau-2013]] The bigger the error, the bigger the swings in a person's daily glucose.[[brazeau-2013]]

So the app lets you use numbers from the pack and measured grams wherever you can. A typed description is the starting point, not the final word.

Go deeper: why scan barcodes?

A packaged food's label already states its carbohydrate. In Australia a nutrition information panel shows energy and six nutrients, including carbohydrate and sugars, both per serving and per 100 g, and its "carbohydrate" includes starches and sugars.[[fsanz-nip]] Scanning pulls those numbers in, so you don't have to type them or guess.

Where do the numbers come from? The app looks in My foods (products you have already checked), then your household food bank, then Open Food Facts, a free public database built by its contributors. Its own terms say it may contain errors and its data is indicative only and must not be used for medical purposes.[[off-terms]] So check the figures against the pack, correct them if needed, and save the product to My foods. From then on, scanning it uses your checked numbers.

Go deeper: why portions and the meal builder?

A label gives carbohydrate per 100 g and per serving. You ate some amount of it. The meal builder does the sum: grams eaten times the carbohydrate per 100 g, divided by 100. A manufacturer chooses its own serving size, and it varies between products,[[fsanz-nip]] so the quarter, half, three-quarter and full buttons use the pack's own serving size, and you can type exact grams instead.

Go deeper: why photo estimates are treated as rough

A study tested two apps on 58 meals. The app that estimated carbohydrate from photos was short by 7.2 g on average, with a typical spread of about 17 g either side, and 30% of its meals were off by more than 20 g. The other app, where the person entered the food and picked from a photo gallery, was closer: 1.4 g off on average.[[joubert-2021]]

Photo-based estimates are a help, not a measurement. That is why this app says plainly when it isn't sure instead of guessing, and why you can always edit the result.

Go deeper: the glycaemic index, and why this app doesn't score foods

The glycaemic index (GI) ranks carbohydrate foods by how much they raise blood glucose. It is measured in at least 10 healthy people eating a portion with 50 g of digestible carbohydrate, with glucose over the next two hours compared against a reference food. A score of 70 or more is high, and 55 or less is low.[[sydney-gi]]

What you eat alongside changes the picture. Fibre, protein, acid such as vinegar or lemon juice, and cooling cooked potato or rice can all lower a food's GI.[[healthdirect-gi]] A 2019 consensus report noted that systematic reviews found GI and glycaemic load had no significant effect on A1C and mixed results on fasting glucose in people with diabetes.[[evert-2019]]

Real meals are mixtures, so this app records the actual meal and shows what your glucose did afterwards, instead of scoring foods.

Go deeper: My foods, the household food bank, and saved meals
  • My foods holds products you've checked against the pack. They are kept on your phone.
  • The household food bank is optional. Once the sync passcode is saved in Settings, your checked products are shared so every device in the household sees the same list. Only product facts leave your phone (barcode, name, carbohydrate, serving size). Your meals never do.
  • Saved meals let you keep a usual lunch or breakfast and load it with one tap, then change anything for today. It is only saved back if you tap Save meal.
