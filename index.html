Import

General information from published medical sources — not personal advice, and not based on your own data. For anything specific to you, confirm with your own doctor. Your own Readings, once there's enough logged history, are the more accurate guide to what actually affects you personally.

Everyday eating

  • Nothing is strictly off-limits — the combination matters more than any single food.
  • Known trigger: a big hit of simple/refined carbs on their own — white bread, white pasta, white rice, pastries, sugary drinks, juice, lollies — especially on an empty stomach.
  • Steadier pattern: pair complex carbs with protein, fat, or fibre — wholegrains, legumes, nuts and seeds, vegetables — to soften the rise so there's less to crash from.
  • Smaller, more frequent meals (roughly every 3 hours) tend to work better than a few large ones.

Alcohol

  • Not all off the table, but there's a real mechanism at play: alcohol makes your liver prioritise breaking it down over topping up blood sugar, so it can trigger or worsen a crash on its own.
  • One study found a specific higher-risk combination: alcohol with a sugary mixer (their example was gin and tonic) — a starch-based drink like beer didn't provoke the same drop.
  • Risk increases alongside other factors — age, other health conditions, exercise around the same time.
  • General takeaway: drink with food rather than on an empty stomach, and be more cautious with sweet mixers and cocktails than plainer drinks.

Eating out & social occasions

  • Check the menu ahead of time so you're not deciding under pressure at the table.
  • Grilled, baked, or steamed on a balanced plate tends to beat fried or sugar-heavy dishes — most places will happily swap a side.
  • Don't arrive starving — skipping a meal before a dinner out is a common setup for overeating simple carbs and crashing afterward.

Glucose warning thresholds

Left blank on purpose — nothing gets colored until you set your own numbers here. For reference while you're deciding (confirm with your doctor, this app isn't recommending a value): the ADA defines hypoglycemia as below 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL).

You can set this higher than 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) for extra personal margin — same idea as driving to your own lower speed limit — but this app won't let it go lower than that standard alert point.

Pulls roughly the last 12 hours of Libre readings through LibreLinkUp. Readings already saved are skipped, so syncing often is fine. The passcode stops anyone else who finds this site from reading your glucose.

Unexplained rise & drop questions

Uses your Libre (CGM) readings only, since finger-pricks are too far apart to show a rise. For drops the app looks back 4 hours, because a reactive low usually follows a meal by 2–4 hours. These are just triggers for a question. They aren't medical limits.
