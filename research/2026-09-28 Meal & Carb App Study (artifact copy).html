How the big diet apps build meals and count carbs

Twelve apps on both the App Store and Google Play, free and paid, from the most downloaded down to the specialists that matter for you: an Australian food database and a bariatric tracker. For each: how meals and carbs work, the pros and cons, and what your app should take or avoid.

Five findings
Big databases are messy

MyFitnessPal's is about 70% member-submitted. The same food can differ by 30%, and a 2024 study found 37% of popular entries off by more than 20%. That's why your app lets the pack win.

Barcodes are going behind paywalls

MyFitnessPal moved barcode scanning to Premium in 2026. Cronometer, Lose It!, Yazio and Carb Manager keep it free. Free scanning is a real selling point.

"Recipe + servings" is the standard

Everyone builds a recipe from ingredients, divides by a number of servings, then logs "1 serving". Nobody does quarter-serves well for small stomachs.

Photo AI is inconsistent

Asking AI the same meal photo repeatedly gave carb answers far enough apart to cause a hypo. It's helpful as a starting guess, never as the number.

Bariatric apps skip carbs

Baritastic focuses on protein, water, vitamins and a bite timer. Carbs and the glucose after a meal, which is your need, are not its focus.

Australia has a verified option

Easy Diet Diary uses 45,000 Australian foods from official data and shares diaries with dietitians. It's small (100K+ Android installs) and lower-rated.

What the money buys

Across the paid tiers, the same five things recur: logging by voice, logging by photo, importing a recipe from a website, repeating a usual meal in one tap, and charts linking food to health numbers.

Closest rival: SNAQ

Meals on the CGM curve, like your Meals tab. It's built for insulin users and leads with photo AI. Your difference: lows first, pack figures over AI guesses, sleeve-sized portions, and it's free.

Voice logging fits you

Three paid apps now sell "say what you ate". You dictate most things already, and your app's AI text parsing is halfway there. A microphone button on the food box would finish it.

App by app

Pros, cons, and what we take

MyFitnessPal

100M+ · 4.3 · iOS + Android
Pros
  • Finds almost anything
  • Import a recipe from a web link, then set servings
  • A recipe logs as one line with totals
Cons
  • Many conflicting entries for one product
  • Barcode and recipe import now paid
  • Nothing about glucose afterwards

Take: "save this meal to log again". Avoid: an unverified crowd database with no way to tell which entry is right.

Carb Manager

5M+ · 4.7 · iOS + Android
Pros
  • Carbs are the headline number
  • Fast, free barcode scanning
  • Logs glucose and ketones too
Cons
  • A dozen versions of one creamer with different carbs
  • Meal planner crashes and loses work (user reports)
  • Keto-focused, not lows

Take: carbs first, glucose alongside. Avoid: letting duplicate entries pile up. One barcode should mean one entry, as in your food bank.

Cronometer

Installs not read · iOS + Android
Pros
  • Verified data only (USDA, NCCDB, manufacturers)
  • Recipes, barcode and custom foods free
  • Very detailed nutrients
Cons
  • US-centred data for fresh foods
  • Busy screens for everyday use

Take: show where a figure came from (pack, food bank, public database). Avoid: screens full of numbers you don't need.

Lose It!

10M+ · 4.3 · iOS + Android
Pros
  • Simplest logging; one-minute setup
  • "My Foods" for things you eat often
Cons
  • Photo logging matches single foods, weak on mixed plates
  • Photo feature is paid

Take: a personal "my foods" list, which you now have. Avoid: presenting a photo guess as the answer.

Yazio · FatSecret

50M+ each · iOS + Android
Pros
  • Free barcode scanning
  • Saved meals and recipes
  • Yazio adds a fasting timer
Cons
  • Mixed-quality databases
  • Plans and recipes behind Pro
  • Weight-loss framing throughout

Take: quick "log this meal again". Avoid: weight-loss scores and streaks, which don't suit a medical tracker.

SNAQ

Paid, from US$2.66/mo · iOS + Android · diabetes
Pros
  • Each meal shown on the CGM curve
  • "How similar meals moved your numbers"
  • Photo, barcode, label and voice logging
Cons
  • Carbs from photo AI, which varies between tries
  • Built around insulin dosing, not lows
  • Subscription

Take: "similar meals" comparisons from your own history (descriptive, not advice). Avoid: photo AI as the carb number.

MyNetDiary

5M+ · 4.7 · iOS + Android
Pros
  • Verified, ad-free data
  • Diabetes add-on with glucose and meds
Cons
  • Diabetes features are an extra subscription
  • US-centred

Take: food and glucose on one timeline, which your Meals tab already does. Avoid: charging for the medical part.

Easy Diet Diary

100K+ · 3.6 · iOS + Android · Australian
Pros
  • 45,000 Australian foods from official data
  • Add a missing product by scanning it
  • Share the diary with your dietitian
Cons
  • Lower ratings; dated feel
  • No glucose link

Take: share with your dietitian (planned: viewers). The official Australian food composition data (AUSNUT) could fill loose foods like fruit and bread later. Avoid: a heavy, form-first interface.

Baritastic

500K+ · 4.6 · iOS + Android · bariatric
Pros
  • Built around surgery stages
  • Bite timer; separate food and water
  • Protein and vitamin reminders
Cons
  • Carbs and glucose aren't the focus
  • Clinic-linked features vary by program

Take: small-portion thinking. Your ¼ and ½ serve buttons are that, applied to carbs. Avoid: nothing; it's a different job.

mySugr

5M+ · 4.4 · iOS + Android
Pros
  • Friendly diabetes log
  • Meter connections
Cons
  • No food database: you work out the carbs
  • Lows aren't the focus

Take: nothing new beyond the earlier study. Avoid: making users do the carb maths.

For your app

Where you stand, and what I'd build next

Have

Barcode scanning, free. Meal builder from ingredients with a carb total. The pack's figures win over any database. Your own saved foods, shared through the household food bank. ¼, ½, ¾ and full serves.

Have

What nobody else does: each meal shown with what your glucose did over the next 5 hours, lows flagged against your own threshold.

Next

Saved meals. Save a built meal ("my usual breakfast") and log it again in one tap, adjusting portions. Every top app has this.

Next

Recipes with servings. Build a dish once (a casserole), say it makes 6 serves, then log ¼ of a serve. This is where small portions matter most.

Next

Show where each figure came from (pack, food bank, public database) on every ingredient, like Cronometer's verified tick.

Next

Voice logging. A microphone button on the food box: say "half a cup of oats with 250 ml milk", and the existing AI parse fills the builder for you to check. It suits how you already work.

Later

"Similar meals" on the Meals tab: the last few times you had something like this, what your glucose did. Facts from your own data, never a recommendation.

Later

Loose foods without barcodes (fruit, bread rolls, rice by the cup) from the official Australian food data, so they don't have to be typed.

Never

A photo-AI carb number used as the answer, a "safe amount to eat", or dose suggestions. The research backs your rule: AI answers vary enough to cause a hypo.
